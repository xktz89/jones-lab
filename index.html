Our Vision

Understanding muscarinic acetylcholine receptors in the brain

"Our research seeks to characterize novel ligands and assess their in vivo efficacy."

The muscarinic acetylcholine receptor (mAChR) family represents an important therapeutic target for numerous clinical indications, from muscarinic antagonists for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, to tolterodine and trospium for overactive bladder and scopolamine for motion sickness.

By understanding the fundamental biology of these receptors and tailoring the pharmacological properties of novel molecular entities, we can develop muscarinic receptor-based treatments for neurological and psychiatric diseases including schizophrenia, Parkinson's Disease, Alzheimer's Disease, and addiction. A new array of clinical programs are currently focused on M1/M4 orthosteric agonists and positive allosteric modulators, with multiple compounds entering clinical trials.

Our group provides the in vivo characterization of novel mAChR subtype-specific ligands for the ongoing development of novel hit and lead molecules and preclinical efficacy for potential clinical candidates for CNS disorders.

Research

Four primary disease areas

01

Schizophrenia

This debilitating psychiatric conditions affects 3 million adults in the U.S. While most antipsychotics target dopamine D2 receptors, leading to adverse side effects, muscarinic-targeted modulation engage brain circuits in a more selective manner.

02

Alzheimer's Disease

With no known cure, there is an urgent need to develop efficacious treatments for memory loss in the 6.9 million people over the age of 65 who suffer from this disease.

03

Depression

Preclinical evidence suggests muscarinic antagonists may reduce depression symptomology.

04

Substance Use Disorders

Over 26 million people worldwide suffer from opioid use disorder. Our work seeks to identify and characterize novel target mechanisms for the prevention of opioid misuse.

Understanding the Role of the M5-receptor Biology in the Mesocorticolimbic Reward Circuitry

The muscarinic subtype 5 receptor (M5) is a modulator of opioid rewarding effects and can decrease symptoms of morphine withdrawal without impacting pain-blocking effects. Our prior work suggests that the M5-receptor is localized within the mesocorticolimbic reward circuitry. In rodent models, highly selective M5-receptor NAMs and an orthosteric inhibitor can block the rewarding and reinforcing effects of μ-opioid agonists without affecting opioid-induced analgesia, prevent the acquisition of oxycodone self-administration, and the reinstatement of μ-opioid agonist drug seeking behaviors. We aim to understand how the M5-receptor regulates dopaminergic transmission and optimize the pharmacological properties of M5-receptor ligands.

Our Role

Our Work in Preclinical Development

The Jones Lab works at the preclinical development stage. After medicinal chemists have optimized a compound, we test it in living systems to answer one question: does this molecule do what we hope in the brain, and is it safe enough to move toward the clinic?

We assess novel mAChR subtype-selective ligands in rodent models relevant to neuropsychiatric disorders. Our data help decide which hit and lead molecules move forward as clinical candidates.

Behavioral Pharmacology

Rodent models of cognition, psychosis-like behavior, reward, and drug self-administration.

Target Engagement

Confirming that compounds reach and act on the intended receptor subtype in vivo.

PK / PD Relationships

Relating drug exposure in brain and plasma to behavioral and physiological effects.

Safety & Tolerability

Screening for motor, cholinergic, and other adverse effects that could limit clinical use.

Principal Investigator

Carrie K. Jones, Ph.D.

Director, In Vivo Pharmacology and Drug Development, Warren Center for Neuroscience Drug Discovery · Associate Professor, Pharmacology

Dr. Jones received her Ph.D. at Indiana University School of Medicine under Dr. Harlan Shannon, a senior scientist at Eli Lilly and Company. For her graduate research, she determined a critical role for the muscarinic cholinergic system in the regulation of normal sensory information processing functions known to be disrupted in individuals with schizophrenia. Prior to joining the faculty at Vanderbilt, she also served as an in vivo pharmacologist at Eli Lilly and Company on several scientific teams focused on the development of novel therapeutics for schizophrenia and chronic pain. Her work contributed to four clinical candidates, including the M1/M4 preferring muscarinic receptor agonist Xanomenline for schizophrenia, the balanced serotonergic and noradrenergic reuptake inhibitor duloxetine, the mixed AMPA/kaniate receptor antagonist LY293558, and an iGluR5 antagonist for chronic pain. Her characterization of duloxetine in preclinical models of inflammatory and persistent pain directly contributed to the ongoing drug discovery effort for this molecule that culminated with the approval of Cymbalta® (duloxetine HCl) for the treatment of chronic pain and depression associated with painful diabetic neuropathy and fibromyalgia.

She is the founding Director of In Vivo and Translational Pharmacology for the Warren Center for Neuroscience Drug Discovery, and current Senior Director of In Vivo Pharmacology and Drug Development. Her work seeks to understand the role of different muscarinic acetylcholine receptor subtypes in the modulation of neuronal circuitry and behaviors relevant to schizophrenia, Alzheimer’s disease, and addiction.

Work With Us

The Jones Lab is dedicated to training the next generation of pharmacologists and neuroscientists. We provide a rigorous, supportive environment where trainees gain expertise in various in vivo pharmacology techniques and develop their scientific independence.

We particularly encourage applications from candidates with backgrounds in neuroscience, pharmacology, molecular biology, genetics, and psychology. Experience with rodent behavior, in vivo recording, or molecular techniques is a plus.
